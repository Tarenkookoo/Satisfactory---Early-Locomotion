## 1. Block signalling — V
- Track is divided into **blocks**; trains cannot collide if they never share a block. Normally **one train per block**. Block length depends on speed, gradient, braking, sighting distance and reaction time. Fixed blocks start and end at signals (signal systems) or stations (token systems). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_signalling (Block signalling, Fixed block).
- **Absolute block** (British): adjacent signal boxes use bell codes and a block instrument. Rear box *offers* the train; advance box *accepts* if the section is clear ("line clear"); rear box clears its signal; "train entering section"; advance box sends "train out of section" once the train has passed complete (tail lamp seen); section is then free for the next train. Sources: https://www.svrwiki.com/Absolute_Block (procedure, one train per section — V); the instrument's three states (normal / line clear / train on line) come from a search summary of mainline sources via https://railsigns.uk/info/ablock1.html (V, secondary; not opened directly). Absolute block became mandatory in the UK after the Regulation of Railways Act 1889 (V, Wikipedia above).
- Trains need the block ahead clear plus an **overlap** for stopping distance (V, Wikipedia above). The deck keeps this simple: not shown as a rule.
- **Interlocking** prevents unsafe combinations, e.g. a signal cannot be cleared when its points are not set (V, same page). Early interlocking was mechanical.

## 2. Semaphore signals — V (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semaphore_signal)
- Arm **horizontal = most restrictive (stop/danger)**. Clear in current British practice = arm at 45°. Upper-quadrant arms rise; lower-quadrant arms drop. Upper-quadrant is safer because gravity returns the arm to danger if a wire breaks.
- **Fail-safe:** a wire (UK) runs from the box lever to the signal; a counterweight returns the wire to normal when the lever is put back, so the signal returns to danger. Broken linkage → arm falls to horizontal.
- **Stop signal:** red arm with a contrasting stripe/spot; protects junctions, points, platforms, block sections; danger compels a stop.
- **Distant signal:** yellow arm with a notched "fishtail" end; warns of the next stop signal; does **not** compel a stop; horizontal = "caution, next signal may be at danger"; clear = all the stop signals controlled from that box are clear. Must be at least one braking distance before the stop signal; interlocking stops a distant clearing unless the stops are clear.
- Night: lamp behind a lens. Red for stop (arm on), yellow for distant (arm on), green when off (clear).
- Combined stop + distant on one post is possible (distant under the stop arm).
- Project reference (repo): the Semaphore pack leaves **upper vs lower quadrant undecided** (`Asset References/RAILWAY_INFRASTRUCTURE_REFERENCE_LIBRARY.md`). The deck must not choose; label "arm style undecided".

## 3. Signal boxes — V (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_box redirected to the "Signalling control" article)
- A signal box (UK) / interlocking tower (North America) is the control point; the **signaller** (signalman) operates it. First box: London & Croydon Railway, 1843. Boxes were often raised for a good view.
- **Lever frame:** levers connect by wires or rods to signals and points; interlocking ensures levers are worked in a safe order. Levers often colour-coded: red for stop signals, black for points (Wikipedia). Fuller example from the repo's Signal Box brief (`Asset References/Signal Boxes/REFERENCE_BRIEF.md`, Bluebell frame): red stop/home, yellow distant, black points, blue locks, brown releases. **Not a universal palette**; the deck labels it "an example".
- Block instruments and bells pass line-clear information between boxes (V).
- Most boxes are now replaced by control centres; some remain on heritage railways (V).

## 4. How a steam locomotive works — V (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_locomotive)
- Firebox burns coal (or coke, oil) and heats water in the **boiler**; steam collects above the water. Water turned to steam expands about 1,600–1,700 times.
- Steam passes through the **regulator** to the **cylinders**; pistons are pushed back and forth; **connecting rods** drive the main wheels and **coupling rods** link the other driving wheels.
- Exhaust steam goes up the **chimney** (blastpipe); that pulls air through the fire (the "blast").
- **Tender** (separate wagon) carries coal and water; **tank engines** carry water in side tanks and coal in a bunker.
- **Why water runs out first:** the steam is released after one use (open cycle), so the boiler needs constant fresh water; the page gives no standard coal:water ratio. The design says water should *generally* be the tighter limit (Agreed). Do not show a ratio.
- Flanged wheels keep a train on the rails: **U** (general knowledge, not fetched). Safe to show; low risk.

## 5. Water and coal supply — V (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_crane; search summary of https://www.american-rails.com/coaling.html, https://nnry.com/?p=3930)
- **Water crane / water column:** upright steel pipe with a swinging arm that rests parallel to the track; delivers up to ~10 m³ (about 2,600 US gal) per minute; supplied by gravity from a tank (water tower) or stream. Usually at the platform end. (V)
- **Coaling:** coal must start higher than the tender so gravity can fill it: a **ramp over a tipple** (coal stage) or a **coaling tower**; small sites hand-shovelled into bunkers; engine sheds often coaled at the end of the day. (V via search summary; not opened in full)
- Repo photos show a coal stage, tubs and a coaling tower (Didcot, Carnforth). Carnforth's tower is LMS-era; Didcot's is GWR-style: keep them as examples, not one place.

## 6. Two-rail track — V (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_(rail_transport))
- Track = rails, fasteners, **sleepers** (ties), ballast. Sleepers spread the load and hold the rails the right distance apart (gauge). Ballast = crushed stone for support and drainage. UK bullhead rail sits in cast-iron **chairs** held by a key. Standard gauge **1,435 mm** (4 ft 8½ in), used by about 60% of world railways (2017).
- Project note: the mod's track gauge is **provisional** (1,435 mm calibration prototype). Do not state it as final.

## 8. LMS Fowler Dock Tank — V (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LMS_Fowler_Dock_Tank)
- 0-6-0T side-tank shunter; **10 built 1928–29** at Derby; short 9 ft 6 in wheelbase for dock curves; **none preserved**. The 3F "Jinty" is a different, much larger class (422 built). Hence no Dock Tank photos exist in the repo; the Small slide uses a Jinty photo with an explicit "related engine" caption. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LMS_Fowler_Class_3F)

## 9. Explainer-deck practice not already in `Reporting/Research/PRESENTATION_DESIGN_RESEARCH.md`
(That file already covers accessibility, reading order, restraint, motion.) New, kept to what applies to a no-presenter newcomer deck:
- **Mayer's multimedia principles** (V via https://aace.org/review/designing-online-learning-spaces-3-a-picture-is-worth-a-thousand-words-a-summary-of-mayers-12-principles-of-multimedia-learning/ and https://repository.chds.hsph.harvard.edu/repository/3755/): coherence (cut extras), signalling (arrows, highlights), contiguity (labels next to the thing), segmenting (short sections), **pre-training** (introduce key terms first). Applied: primer and glossary come before the mod; every diagram labelled in place; sections announced by a divider strip.
- **Slidedocs** (Duarte, V via https://www.duarte.com/training/slide-document-design/slidedocs/ summaries): a deck meant to be *read* may carry more text than a talk slide; one key message per slide; grid, white space, consistent visual language; an interactive contents list. Applied with a **tighter cap than Duarte's ~100 words** because the owner asked for visuals over words (~25 words per slide).
- The 3-second "billboard" test is for projected slides, not read-alone decks (V, same search); not applied.
- Navigation: a contents slide with hyperlinks is optional (Proposed for Stage 2 only if cheap; not required).
- **U:** no study was found giving an ideal slide count for a read-alone explainer. 42 slides is the owner's range.
